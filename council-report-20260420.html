→ The Recommendation

Go all-in on Reputation Manager, but change the entry point. Do not lead with "give me access to your Google Business Profile." Lead with a done-for-you sample. Find 3–5 London pubs with unanswered reviews, write professional draft replies for their specific real reviews, and send those drafts cold — no ask for access upfront. The offer is: "I'll do this for you every week. Here's what it looks like. Free for 14 days, £55 after that, and you post them yourself until you trust me."

On the Greene King network: use it in week one or don't complain about cold outreach conversion. The message is not "can you be my client" — it's "I'm building something and I'd love 20 minutes to see if I've got the product right." That framing works because it's true and low-stakes.

On location: be transparent if asked. Frame it as temporary: "I'm back in London in [month] — if you want to meet in person before committing, happy to do that then." Stop LinkedIn for 90 days. It's a 6-month game.

🔴 The Contrarian

The fatal flaw is the buying cycle, not the product. Pub owners drowning in admin don't browse their inbox looking for SaaS subscriptions. They're behind the bar at 7pm reading your cold email between pulling pints and arguing with a supplier. The decision-maker is also the floor manager, the stock-taker, and the person whose cousin just quit. Your sales cycle assumption is broken.

£55/month sounds cheap but it's a recurring commitment. A cash-strapped pub owner in 2026 UK hospitality — post-energy crisis, post-wage increases — sees "cancel anytime" and reads "another thing I'll forget to cancel." Friction isn't the setup fee. It's the mental overhead of a new vendor relationship.

26 unanswered reviews proves pain, not willingness to pay. They're not answering reviews because they don't have time and don't believe it moves revenue. You need to prove the link between replied reviews and covers. Without that, you're selling peace of mind to someone who's already made peace with the chaos.

The 3-month constraint is the real problem. At £55/mo, you need 18 paying clients to hit £1k MRR. From zero, in 90 days, via cold outreach, to one of the UK's most relationship-driven industries. The maths only work if your conversion rate is exceptional.

The Greene King angle is your actual asset — and you're not using it aggressively enough. One warm intro from a former colleague to a regional ops manager beats 200 cold emails. If you're not mining that network hard in week one, the product doesn't matter.

🟡 The Outsider

The 26-out-of-30 stat is doing a lot of heavy lifting here. Unanswered reviews aren't the same as "willing to pay £55/month to fix it." That gap is the entire business risk, and it's being glossed over.

From outside: pub owners who haven't replied to reviews in months may not think it's a problem. They might know and not care. Or they're stretched so thin that adding a new subscription feels like one more thing to manage, not one less.

The pricing looks like it was built for ease of selling, not for what the market will bear. £55/month is suspiciously low — either it's priced to remove friction (smart) or it signals low confidence in the value.

The Greene King experience is genuinely useful, but it's operations experience at a large corporate chain. A single independent East London pub is a completely different beast. The owner is the manager is the bartender. Decision cycles are not the same.

The harder question nobody's asking: is a pub owner in East London going to give a stranger based in India admin access to their Google Business profile? Trust is the actual product here, not the automation.
